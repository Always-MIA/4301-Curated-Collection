# Psychological Health & Trauma

Flight attendants can encounter situations that extend far beyond ordinary workplace stress. Medical emergencies, deaths, severe passenger incidents, evacuations, injuries, and other unexpected events can have lasting psychological effects. This section focuses on psychological distress, mental-health concerns, and the effects of traumatic experiences in aviation.

---

## Common Mental Disorders Among Civil Aviation Flight Attendants

**Source:** Feijó, D., Luiz, R. R., & Câmara, V. M. (2014). _Common Mental Disorders Among Civil Aviation Flight Attendants._
**Type:** Peer-Reviewed Research

**What it covers:** This study examined the prevalence of common mental disorders among 453 Brazilian civil aviation flight attendants. Researchers found that 29.8% of participants met the study's criteria for common mental disorders. The study also examined factors associated with these conditions, including sex, time since training, physical activity, and flight routes.

**Why it is valuable:** The study provides quantitative evidence that psychological health concerns are present within the flight-attendant population. It also demonstrates that occupational mental health can be influenced by both workplace organization and individual circumstances.

**Who would benefit:** Flight attendants, aviation students, researchers, healthcare professionals, and people interested in the prevalence of mental-health concerns among aviation workers would benefit from this research.

**Limitations:** The study was conducted in Brazil and used a cross-sectional design and self-report questionnaire, so its findings may not directly represent flight attendants in the United States.

## Integrating Qualitative Methods into Occupational Health Research: A Study of Women Flight Attendants

**Source:** Ballard, T. J., et al. (2004). _Integrating Qualitative Methods into Occupational Health Research: A Study of Women Flight Attendants._
**Type:** Peer-Reviewed Qualitative Research

**What it covers:** This study used focus groups and individual interviews with current and former women flight attendants to identify work-related sources of psychosocial stress. Participants discussed relationships with passengers, coworkers, and supervisors, work-family compatibility, isolation, workplace violence, and concerns about mental health.

**Why it is valuable:** Unlike large surveys that reduce experiences to numbers, this research captures how flight attendants actually describe their workplace experiences. It adds an important human perspective to the collection and helps explain some of the stressors identified in larger quantitative studies.

**Who would benefit:** Flight attendants, students, researchers, airline managers, and anyone interested in the lived experience of cabin crew would find this source useful.

**Limitations:** Only 26 participants were included, and the study focused on women, so its findings cannot represent every flight attendant. The research also reflects the working environment of the study population at the time it was conducted.

[Learn More](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14739383/)

## PTSD Reactions and Functioning of American Airlines Flight Attendants in the Wake of September 11

**Source:** Lating, J. M., Sherman, M. F., Lowry, J. L., Everly, G. S., & Peragine, T. (2004). _PTSD Reactions and Functioning of American Airlines Flight Attendants in the Wake of September 11._
**Type:** Peer-Reviewed Research

**What it covers:** This study examined psychological reactions among American Airlines flight attendants following the September 11 attacks. Researchers surveyed approximately 26,000 flight attendants and received 2,050 responses. About 18.2% of respondents reported symptoms consistent with probable PTSD.

**Why it is valuable:** This source is particularly relevant because it documents the psychological effects of a major traumatic event on flight attendants working for an airline directly involved in the attacks. It demonstrates how aviation employees can experience occupational trauma that continues after the immediate event.

**Who would benefit:** Flight attendants, aviation students, trauma researchers, airline organizations, and anyone studying the psychological effects of aviation disasters would benefit from this source.

**Limitations:** The study focuses on an extraordinary historical event and cannot be used to represent the everyday mental-health experiences of all flight attendants. It also reflects a specific historical period.

[Learn More](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15167408/)

## Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in Airplane Cabin Crew Attendants

**Source:** Marks, M., Yule, W., & de Silva, P. (1995). _Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in Airplane Cabin Crew Attendants._
**Type:** Peer-Reviewed Research

**What it covers:** This study examined six cabin crew members who survived an airplane crash that killed 47 passengers. Participants were interviewed eight months after the crash and again later, with questionnaires assessing intrusive thoughts, avoidance, depression, anxiety, fear, and other psychological symptoms. All six participants met the study's criteria for PTSD.

**Why it is valuable:** The study provides a clear example of how severe aviation trauma can continue to affect cabin crew long after an incident occurs. It also emphasizes the importance of preparation before traumatic events and appropriate support afterward.

**Who would benefit:** Flight attendants, aviation safety professionals, trauma researchers, mental-health professionals, and students studying occupational trauma would benefit from this source.

**Limitations:** The sample included only six people involved in one crash, making it impossible to generalize the results to the wider flight-attendant population. The study is also relatively old.

[Learn More](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7661839/)
