# Occupational Stress & Work Environment

Working as a flight attendant involves much more than serving passengers and following safety procedures. Flight attendants work in an environment where schedules, workload, passenger interactions, workplace relationships, and time away from home can create ongoing stress. This section focuses on the workplace conditions that can influence the mental health and quality of life of aviation employees.

---

## Job Stress in Aircrew

**Source:** Centers for Disease Control and Prevention / National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (CDC/NIOSH). (2024). _Job Stress in Aircrew._  
**Type:** Government resource  

**What it covers:** This CDC/NIOSH resource explains the different types of job stress that aircrew may experience. It identifies heavy job demands, long and irregular hours, sleep disruption, unpredictable schedules, difficult passengers or coworkers, time away from home, and traumatic events as potential stressors. It also distinguishes between acute, chronic, and traumatic incident stress and discusses ways employers and employees can reduce the effects of workplace stress.

**Why it is valuable:** This is one of the strongest introductory sources in the collection because it connects many separate issues in aviation to the broader concept of occupational stress. It also emphasizes that workplace conditions and organizational decisions can influence employee well-being.

**Who would benefit:** Flight attendants, aviation students, airline managers, researchers, and anyone trying to understand aviation-related workplace stress would benefit from this resource.

**Limitations:** The information applies broadly to aircrew rather than specifically to flight attendants, so some recommendations may be more relevant to pilots or other aviation workers.

[Learn More](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/aviation/prevention/aircrew-stress.html)

## Job Stress Among Female Flight Attendants

**Source:** MacDonald, L. A., Deddens, J. A., Grajewski, B. A., Whelan, E. A., & Hurrell, J. J. (2003). _Job Stress Among Female Flight Attendants._  
**Type:** Peer-reviewed research  

**What it covers:** This study examined chronic job stress among 73 female flight attendants working for two commercial airlines. Researchers looked at relationships between workplace stressors, psychological distress, fatigue, and job dissatisfaction. The findings showed that job stressors had a meaningful effect on psychological and work-related outcomes even after individual factors were considered.

**Why it is valuable:** This source provides direct research evidence connecting flight-attendant working conditions with psychological well-being. It is especially useful alongside the CDC/NIOSH source because it moves from a broad description of aviation stressors to data collected directly from flight attendants.

**Who would benefit:** Flight attendants, aviation students, occupational-health researchers, and anyone interested in how workplace conditions affect employee satisfaction would find this study useful.

**Limitations:** The sample included only 73 women from two airlines, so the findings may not represent male flight attendants or employees working for other airlines and regions.

[Learn More](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12855911/)

## Psychosocial Work Environment and Medical Symptoms Among Swedish Commercial Airline Cabin Crew

**Source:** Wahlstedt, K., Lindgren, T., Norbäck, D., Wieslander, G., & Runeson, R. (2010). _Psychosocial Work Environment and Medical Symptoms Among Swedish Commercial Airline Cabin Crew._  
**Type:** Peer-reviewed research  

**What it covers:** This study examined the relationship between psychosocial working conditions and health symptoms among 918 flight attendants, stewards, and pursers at one Swedish airline. Researchers considered factors such as job demands, control, social support, and work-related strain. The study found that symptoms including fatigue, headaches, concentration difficulties, and gastrointestinal problems were more common in high-strain work situations.

**Why it is valuable:** The study demonstrates that the work environment can affect employees beyond their emotional state. It also highlights social support as an important part of the relationship between workplace conditions and health.

**Who would benefit:** Flight attendants, airline managers, occupational-health researchers, and students studying workplace stress would benefit from this source.

**Limitations:** The research was conducted at one Swedish airline and relied on questionnaire responses, so its findings may not fully represent flight attendants in other countries or airline environments.

[Learn More](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20306495/)

## Estimating the Health Consequences of Flight Attendant Work

**Source:** McNeely, E., Mordukhovich, I., Tideman, S., Gale, S., & Coull, B. (2018). _Estimating the Health Consequences of Flight Attendant Work: Comparing Flight Attendant Health to the General Population in a Cross-Sectional Study._  
**Type:** Peer-reviewed research 

**What it covers:** This study compared the health of 5,366 U.S. flight attendants with a general-population sample of 2,729 people. Researchers found higher prevalence of several conditions among flight attendants, including sleep disorders, fatigue, and depression. The study also examined whether certain health outcomes increased with longer time working as a flight attendant.

**Why it is valuable:** This source provides a large-scale look at the broader health consequences associated with flight-attendant work. It helps demonstrate that occupational aviation issues can extend beyond temporary stress and affect multiple areas of health.

**Who would benefit:** Flight attendants, aviation professionals, researchers, students, and people interested in the long-term effects of aviation careers would find this study useful.

**Limitations:** The research is cross-sectional, meaning it cannot establish that flight-attendant work directly caused the health conditions. The authors also note the possibility of recall bias.

[Learn More](https://bmcpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12889-018-5221-3)
